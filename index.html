Our story

Rescue robots are built to save lives — yet the ground at a disaster site stops them. Wheels get stuck on rubble. Tracks jam on broken steps. Watching people step over obstacles, we asked a simple question: what if a robot could lift its legs too? So we designed, printed and coded one. We called it RISE.

The problem
0hrs

After an earthquake or collapse, rescuers race against the golden 72 hours — the window when trapped survivors are most likely to be saved.

Disaster sites are ruins, debris and narrow gaps. Traditional wheeled and tracked robots get stuck, and every minute stuck is a minute lost. Sending people in instead puts rescuers in danger.

Option A

Wheels only

  • Stuck on steps & debris
  • Tips on side slopes
Option B

Legs only

  • Flexible over obstacles

  • Slow and power-hungry
RISE

Wheels + adaptive legs

  • Wheel speed on the flat
  • Lifts legs to stay level
  • Simple, cheap, printable
The innovation

Its body stays level — whatever the ground does.

BODY TILT 0.0°LEFT LEG 0 mmRIGHT LEG 0 mm● LEVEL
01 — DRIVE

Rolls like a wheel

On flat ground RISE rolls on two grippy wheels. It checks its balance 200 times a second, so it stays upright while moving fast.

02 — THE PROBLEM

One wheel hits an obstacle

A normal robot just tilts. Its camera points the wrong way, anything it carries slides off, and it can tip over or get stuck.

03 — ADAPT

Legs rise and fall

A tilt sensor notices the lean. Small motors in the legs lower one side and raise the other until the body is flat again.

04 — SEE & FOLLOW

Recognises and follows

Its AI camera recognises a person, and RISE follows them by itself, keeping them on camera while the legs deal with the ground.

Mode 01

Remote control

Someone drives RISE with a wireless remote — forward, back, turn, stop — and the legs keep it level automatically.

Mode 02

Autonomous follow

RISE locks onto a person and follows on its own, filming from different angles as it moves. Because the body stays level, the video stays steady.

Mechanical design

Designed in Fusion 360.
Printed, not machined.

Every structural part is 3D-printed — light, cheap, and repairable in the field. A broken part can simply be reprinted.

Validation

Four experiments.
Four passes.

Flat ground and foam boards of different thickness stood in for steps and uneven rubble.

Test 01

Different height differences

We drove it over flat ground, a thin foam board and a thick one to check the legs adjust.

✓ PASSED — all three surfaces
Test 02

Attitude adjustment

Starting tilted on the foam board, RISE drove onto flat ground and had to return to a level posture within 10 seconds.

✓ PASSED — re-levelled in time
Test 03

Load-bearing

A box was placed on top while RISE adapted across terrain, testing stability under load.

✓ PASSED — carried the load across
Test 04

Cargo transport

RISE had to push a box straight into a marked area — testing its strength and steering.

✓ PASSED — delivered to target

Market & business

Cheap enough to deploy
in numbers.

Advanced rescue robots exist, but they are expensive — so most teams own one or none. RISE takes the opposite approach.

Typical commercial rescue / legged robotUS$10,000+

Printable parts, off-the-shelf electronics — several units for a fraction of the cost.

Customers

Who needs it

Fire and rescue services, emergency-management agencies and volunteer rescue groups — especially in earthquake-prone regions.

Also

Who else benefits

Creators and sports teams who want a robot that follows and films them, robotics labs, and schools teaching control systems.

Edge

Our advantage

Wheels and legs in one, follows people by itself, and broken parts can be reprinted on the spot.

Business model

RISE Edu Kit

Printable parts, electronics and lessons for STEM classes and robotics clubs — builds revenue and a community early.

RISE Field Unit

A tougher, ready-built robot with a better camera, for rescue teams.

RISE Swarm

Groups of robots that work together, plus training.

Go-to-market

Start with schools and robotics competitions, where the kit is useful today. Use that community and feedback to refine the design, then partner with fire-training centres and rescue volunteers for field pilots.
